How access works at NCSA · current state

An award is not access.

Someone approves an allocation. Days or minutes later a researcher can run a job. In between sit about a dozen systems, most of which almost nobody at NCSA has reason to name. This page explains what each one does, in plain language, and shows how the path differs depending on which system the researcher is trying to use.

Who this is for. Anyone at NCSA who keeps hearing “IDDS,” “acctd” or “charge group” and has never been told what they are. It is not an administration guide and not a design proposal — it describes how things work today, as of late 2026.

Five things have to happen

Every path to an NCSA resource — every program, every system — passes through the same five stages. The names of the components change; the stages do not.

Identity runs alongside all five and is not one of them. Having an NCSA account tells a machine who you are; it grants you nothing. See Logins & identity.

The shortest honest description

Three things carry almost all the weight

IDDS is the database that knows what was awarded. acctd is the machinery that delivers it to resources. NCSA LDAP is the directory every service reads at login. If you understand those three and the order they run in, you can follow almost any access question at NCSA.

Two writers, one directory

LDAP is written by two separate things. A sync script running on Savannah writes who you are — your name, email, UID — into the main branch. acctd writes what you are allowed to use into per-resource branches. They do not coordinate, and the per-resource branches have no named owner.

It is a batch job, not an event

Nothing fires the moment an award is approved. Jobs run on a timer and ask “anything new?” This is why access appears some minutes or hours after approval rather than immediately, and it is the single most common source of “it was approved, why can’t I log in.”

Automation is the exception, not the rule

Delta and Hydro are the only NCSA systems where an approval becomes a working project with no human in the loop. Everywhere else — Radiant, Taiga, Granite, Nightingale, the Campus Cluster — a person performs at least one documented step by hand.

Why there is no single sign-on across all of it

They answer different questions

A NetID says you are a member of the University. An NCSA identity says you are known to NCSA’s own systems. An ACCESS ID says you are a researcher registered with a national programme. None of these implies the others, and NCSA’s standing policy is that resources default to NCSA identities only — linking an external identity in is a decision that needs CISO approval.

Nothing reconciles them

There is no service that can answer “this NetID and this ACCESS ID are the same human.” Staff NCSA accounts do get a link to the campus identifier at creation, but that is the only automatic one. Everywhere else the match is made by hand, on name and email — which is also why duplicate accounts for the same person occur and have to be repaired manually.

LDAP and Kerberos are both involved

A complete NCSA identity record is split: LDAP holds the attributes (name, email, UID, group memberships), Kerberos holds the password. This is ordinary for a Unix environment, but it means turning someone off is not a single operation — it touches more than one store.

Where the account actually comes from

NCSA identities are minted by IDDS, which generates the UID and GID. A sync script running on Savannah copies the identity attributes into LDAP. Account creation, password resets and deletions travel through a message queue between the identity web front end and Kerberos — legacy plumbing the Identity Coordination group is working to retire.

Practical consequence. If you are trying to help someone and they say “I can’t log in,” the first useful question is not what’s your username but which system, and which login did you use. Delta over SSH and the Campus Cluster over SSH want credentials from two entirely separate identity domains with two separate Duo enrolments.
